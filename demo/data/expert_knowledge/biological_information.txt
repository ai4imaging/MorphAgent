One of the most central, hallmark image features of pathological Tau protein is a fundamental change in its subcellular localization: a shift from the normal axon-dominated distribution to mislocalized accumulation and aggregation within the soma (cell body) and dendrites. In healthy neurons, Tau is a microtubule-associated protein enriched mainly in axons, where it stabilizes microtubules and supports axonal transport. This localization is highly organized and graded. For example, in mature mouse hippocampal neurons, endogenous Tau-mEOS2 shows strong axonal enrichment with a decreasing concentration gradient along the axon from the soma to the growth cone. Even under physiological conditions its distribution is not perfectly uniform, but follows a non-uniform, sparse pattern along axonal microtubules with a spacing of roughly 200 nm, suggesting that microtubule binding sites are not fully saturated normally. Under disease conditions, however, this precise localization mechanism is disrupted. In vulnerable neuron types, such as Drosophila models expressing human Tau, Tau gradually shifts from a smooth, continuous axonal distribution to diffuse spreading into the dendrites. Likewise, in cortical inhibitory neurons derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells, exposure to pathological Tau seeds causes Tau to accumulate markedly in the soma and dendrites, whereas the corresponding spinal-cord-like neurons show greater resistance. This "migration" or "translocation" from axons to the soma and dendrites is the primary visual indicator of whether Tau pathology has occurred; it directly reflects the loss of Tau's molecular function and an increased tendency toward pathological aggregation.
